                                     FOREWORD
The Central Vigilance Commission has brought out Vigilance Manuals comprising various
rules, instructions, guidelines and judicial pronouncements to facilitate uniformity,
consistency and transparency in handling of vigilance administration from time to time. The
Manual aims to strengthen Preventive Vigilance, promote ethical decision-making and
enhance public confidence in organisations. The first edition of the Vigilance Manual was
published in 1968. The latest (eighth) edition of the Vigilance Manual was brought out in
2021 .

During the last 5 years since 2021, many new developments, policy initiatives and
clarifications have taken place. A number of new guidelines, both by the Government and the
Commission, have been issued. For example, Manuals of Procurement of Goods, Works,
Consultancy and non-Consultancy Services of Department of Expenditure, Ministry of
Finance; Updation of CCS (Pension) Rules; Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), Bharatiya
Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS) and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam have replaced the
Indian Penal Code (IPC), Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC) and the Indian Evidence Act;
fresh / revised instructions / guidelines issued by the Government; changes in guidelines in
Banking and Insurance, etc. The Commission has also consolidated, updated and revised its
earlier guidelines, circulars, public notices, etc. in the form of Master Circulars and Public
Notices.

The need was felt to update the Vigilance Manual accordingly to bring about ease of
reference and consolidate latest provisions and provide a comprehensive reference book for
management as well as those involved in the vigilance administration. Since Commission has
also consolidated the previous guidelines in the form of Master Circulars, the Vigilance
Manual shall be read with the consolidated Master Circulars .

The Manual should serve as ready reference for vigilance functionaries. It is intended to
supplement the applicable statutory provisions, government instructions and guidelines issued
by the competent authorities and should not be construed as a substitute for them.

The Commission is hopeful that this Manual will contribute significantly to strengthening
vigilance administration and fostering a culture of integrity, accountability and excellence
within the Ministries / Departments / Organisations.

INTRODUCTION

fudian history is replete with examples of good governance practices which helped to ensure ethics
in public affairs. The organisations, systems and procedures of the government must not only be
efficient but also ethical, just and fair, with integrity being its essential ingredient, as righteousness
is the foundation of good governance.

The ill-effects of conuption are well known. It undennines developmental efforts and weakens
democratic institutions. Conuption is manifested in various forms such as bribery; nepotism;
willful action or inaction to unduly benefit someone or to deny benefit to someone; favouritism
etc. The challenge before us is to create an environment in which the honest can work fearlessly
and the conupt are penalised promptly.

The battle against conuption is fought on many fronts. An oversight mechanism often referred to
as vigilance administration is at the forefront of this battle. Vigilance is defined as watchfulness
and alertness. Vigilance administration in any organisation is an integral function like any other
function of management, such as finance, personnel, operation, marketing, material, contracts, etc.
Vigilance administration encompasses of preventive and punitive anti-conuption measures. It
includes detecting irregularities, analysing and finding out reasons for such irregularities and
making effective systemic improvements to curb them. It also entails identifying the public
servants responsible for misconduct and taking appropriate punitive action against them. If the
vigilance set-up in an organisation is effective, it will ensure effective functioning of other
segments.

1.1      VIGILANCE ADMINISTRATION IN CENTRAL GOVERNMENT
         Effective vigilance administration mechanism is to be ensured by (i) the Central
         Vigilance Commission (hereinafter referred to as the Commission) (ii) Administrative
         Vigilance Division (A VD) in the Department of Personnel & Training (DoPT); (iii)
         Central Bureau of fuvestigation (CBI); (iv) Vigilance units in the Ministries /
         Departments / Organisations of Government of fudia, (hereinafter referred to as
         Organsation); (v) Disciplinary Authorities; (vi) Supervisory Officers and (vii) Chief
         Executive of the organisation concerned.

1.1.1       ADMINISTRATIVE                      VIGILANCE DIVISION

            The AVD was set up in the Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA), in August 1955, to serve
            as a central agency to undertake overall responsibility for anti-corruption measures. With
            the establishment of the Commission, a good part of the functions, earlier performed by
            A VD, are now exercised by the Commission. The AVD, which is now part ofDoPTin
            the Ministry of Personnel, Public Grievances & Pension, is now responsible for the
            formulation and implementation of policies of Central Government and providing
            guidance to other ministries I departments in matters pertaining to vigilance, integrity in
            public services and anti-corruption measures.

1.1.2       CENTRAL BUREAU OF INVESTIGATION

            The CBI was constituted under the Government of India Resolution No. 4/31/61-T dated
            01.04.1963. CBI is the successor organisation to Delhi Special Police Establishment
            (DSPE) with an enlarged charter of functions. With the establishment of CBI on 1st April,
            1963, DSPE has been made one of its divisions, i.e. 'Investigation and Anti-Corruption
            Division' which derives its police powers from the Delhi Special Police Establishment
            Act, 19461. DSPE is empowered to inquire and to investigate certain specified offences
            or classes of offences pertaining to corruption and other kinds of malpractices involving
            public servants. Section 3 of the DSPE Act-1946 provides that ''the Central Government
            may, by notification in the Official Gazette, specify the offences or class of offences,
            which are to be investigated by the Delhi Special Police Establishment''.

            The Anti-Corruption Division of CBI is responsible for collection of intelligence with
            regard to corruption, maintaining liaison with various organisations through their
            vigilance functionaries, inquiries into complaints about bribery and corruption,
            investigation and prosecution of offences pertaining to bribery and corruption.

            It handles all cases registered under the Prevention of Corruption Act (PC Act), 1988 (as
            amended on 26.07.2018)2 and cases registered under other related sections of the Indian
            Penal Code, 18603. If an offence under any other criminal law is committed along with
            an offence of bribery and / or corruption, it will also be investigated by the Anti-
            Corruption Division. The CBI is headed by a Director who is appointed by the Central
            Government on the recommendation of a high-level committee chaired by the Prime
            Minister.

1.1.3      ROLE   OF THE                           CHIEF      EXECUTIVE                 IN       VIGILANCE
           ADMINISTRATION

           The responsibility of ensuring probity, fairness and transparency in an organisation vests
           with the ChiefExecutive of that organisation. The ChiefVigilance Officer (CVO) assists
           the Chief Executive ineffective vigilance administration and also acts as an extended ann
           of the Commission. A responsibility is cast upon the Chief Executive to set the right tone
           from the top management to ensure that the guilty are punished swiftly and the innocent
           are protected from harassment. This would help prevent misconduct and unethical
           practices, and support the efficient functioning of the organisation . The Chief Executive
           is, therefore, expected to carefully review the vigilance work at least on a quarterly basis
           and act upon the reports submitted by CVO in a timely manner.

           The Chief Executive is required to ensure the following: -

(i)        Filling up vacancies in the Vigilance Unit;
(ii)       Holding structured meeting with the CVO every quarter;
(iii)      Develop monitoring mechanisms to ensure probity and transparency in the organisation;
(iv)       Encourage efforts for effective preventive vigilance mechanism so that occasions for
           resorting to punitive methods are reduced;
(v)        Extend adequate support to surveillance activities of the Vigilance Unit for obtaining
           information on any malpractices and taking corrective action thereon;
(vi)       To facilitate periodic training of all officials about rules, regulations, systems and
           procedures for generating general awareness about possible deviations and transgressions
           attracting sanctions so that officials do not suffer due to ignorance; and
(vii)      To ensure that the Conduct, Discipline & Appeal (CDA) Rules of the organisation
           concerned are updated and brought in accordance with the Model Rules issued by the
           Department of Public Enterprise (DPE) 1 and the government organisations concerned.

1.1.5      DISCIPLINARY AUTHORITY

           The disciplinary authority means the authority who has been entrusted with powers to
           impose any penalty on officials of an organisation under the Discipline & Appeal Rules,
           as may be applicable to the officials of the organisation concerned. In respect of
           employees governed by the Central Civil Services (CCS) Classification, Control and
           Appeal (CCA) Rules,1965, the termDisciplinaryAuthorityis defined as the authority
           competent to impose on a government servant, any of the penalties specified in the CCS
           (CCA) Rules.

           It is the responsibility of the Disciplinary Authority (DA) to ensure discipline in the
           organisation and to deal with misconduct by way of awarding suitable penalty. Role and
           functions of the DA are explained in detail in the Discipline & Appeal Rules of the
           organisation concerned.

1.1.6      CENTRAL VIGILANCE COMMISSION

(i)        Genesis: The Commission was set up by the Government of India by a Resolution
           dated 11.2.19642 in pursuance of the recommendations made by the Committee on
           Prevention of Corruption (commonly known as the San.than.amCommittee). Further, in
           pursuance of the directions of the Hon'ble Supreme Court of India in the case of Vmeet
           Narain vs. Union of India (CWP 340-343 of 1993-1 SCC 226), the Commission was
           accorded statutory status with effect from 25.8.1998 through The Central Vigilance
           Commission Ordinance, 1998.

           This was followed by the Central Vigilance Commission (Amendment) Ordinance
           dated 27.10.1998, the Central Vigilance Commission Ordinance dated 08.01.1999, the
           DoPT Resolution No. 371/20/99-A VDIII dated 04.04.1999 and the DoPT Resolution
           No. 371/20/99-AVD-III dated 13.08.2002, while the Central Vigilance Commission Bill
           was under the consideration of the Parliament. Subsequently, the Central Vigilance
           Commission Bill was passed by both Houses of Parliament in 2003 and the President
           gave assent to the same on 11 th September 2003. Thus, the Central Vigilance
           Commission Act (CVC Act), 2003 (No. 45 of 2003) came into effect from that date.

               CIDEF VIGILANCE OFFICERS



INTRODUCTION

The primary responsibility for maintaining efficiency, integrity and transparency in an organisation
vests in the Chief Executive [Secretary in the case of ministry / department and Chief Executive
Officer (Chairman cum Managing Director I Managing Director etc.) in case of any other
organisation]. Such an authority is assisted by the CVO in performing vigilance functions. The
CVO acts as an advisor to the Chief Executive and reports directly to him. He heads the Vigilance
Unit of the organisation. He also provides a link between his organisation and the Connnission.
Vigilance functions to be performed by the CVO are of wide sweep and include collecting
intelligence about the prevailing corrupt practices or the possibility of corrupt practices by the
officials of the organisation; investigating or causing an investigation to be made into allegations
having vigilance sensitivity reported to him; processing investigation reports for further
consideration of the Competent Authority concerned; referring cases to the Connnission for advice
in accordance with the existing guidelines; taking steps to prevent improper practices or
connnission of misconduct; examining audit, inspection and other reports etc.

2.1          APPOINTMENT OF CHIEF VIGILANCE OFFICER
             The CVOs are appointed by the Competent Authority in the organisations of Government
             of India to cany out vigilance work. Large organisations should have a full-time CVO,
             i.e., the official should not have any other responsibility. If it is considered that the CVO
             in an organisation does not have full-time vigilance work, he may be entrusted with such
             functions that serve as input to vigilance activities. However, the CVO should not be
             involved in decision making processes or in activities of such nature, which are likely to
             have clear vigilance sensitivity.

             Furthermore, in order to be effective in his functioning, a CVO should ordinarily be an
             outsider appointed for a fixed tenure on deputation terms as stipulated by the DoPT vide
             their OM dated 18.11.2022 1. The CVO in an organisation is appointed with prior
             consultation of the Connnission and no person whose appointment in that capacity is
             objected to by the Connnission may be so appointed.

2.2     APPOINTMENT OF THE PART TIME CHIEF VIGILANCE OFFICER
        IN THE MINISTRIES/DEPARTMENTS

        The duties ofCVO in the ministries/ departments of the Government of India may be
        entrusted to an official of that ministry/ department on a part time basis, with the approval

        control of a ministry / department which are small in size and do not have an official of
        sufficient seniority to be appointed as the CVO. In such a case, the ministry/ department
        concerned may entrust vigilance functions to a suitable official of appropriate level. Such
        an appointment can be made under intimation to the Commission.

2.3     ADDITIONAL CHARGE ARRANGEMENTS TO THE CHIEF VIGILANCE
        OFFICER

        A proposal for appointment of the CVOs on part time basis or for giving additional charge
        of the post of the CVO of an organisation to an official already working as CVO in
        another organisation, should be made to the Commission by the ministry / department
        concerned. The following information / details should invariably be forwarded to the
        Commission along with the proposal: -
(i)     Tenure of the official proposed as the CVO (in his present capacity).
(ii)    Is he holding additional charge as the CVO of any other organisations?
(iii)   Is the Head Quarter of current proposed organisation is the same as that of the
        organisation, where he is already working as the CVO?
(iv)    Brief profile of the organisation (number of employees, number of cases arising in a year,
        status, core activity, organisation set up, etc.).
(v)     Order of preference, if the proposed official(s) are from the respective ministry /
        department. Provided, the proposed official(s) do not hold any charge which attracts
        conflict of interest with the proposed post of CVO.
(vi)    Brief profile of the official(s) proposed as CVO.

            AUTONOMOUS BODIES AND DEPARTMENTS

            The following guidelines are prescribed for filling up full-time posts of the CVOs:

(i)         The posts of the CVO in CPSEs and other organisations under administrative control of

(ii)        The DoPT would request the cadre controlling authorities of various organised services

(iii)       An official willing to be posted as the CVO would submit online application as is done

            along with their AP AR dossiers to the DoPT. Such applications would be forwarded by

(v)         The selection of official(s) appearing on the CVO offer list in individual organisation as

2.9         TENURE OF THE CHIEF VIGILANCE OFFICER

            The tenure of CVO in an organisation shall be for a period of 03 years which is
            extendable by another 02 years, subject to overall combined time limit prescribed
            for central deputation and/ or being away from the cadre, as per instruction issued
            by the DoPT from time to time. The initial tenure of 03 years as the CVO in an
            organisation is extendable for a further period of 03 years on lateral transfer to
            another organisation with prior concurrence of the Co1TI1Tiission,subject to the
            overall combined time limit prescribed for central deputation and / or being away
            from the cadre, as per instructions issued by the DoPT from time to time. Once
            an official has worked as the CVO in a particular organisation, he shall not be
            considered for the post of the CVO in the same organisation for another term.

2.10        The officials already on central deputation on a CSS post would normally be
            appointed for a minimum residual tenure of 03 years. Applications of such
            officials should be forwarded with the approval of the Minister-in-charge of the
            ministry/ department in which the official is posted and should reach the DoPT
            at least a year before the expiry of tenure of the official on the CSS post.

             PRELIMINARY ENQUIRY


 INTRODUCTION

 Preliminary Enquiry is conducted for verifying the allegations made in a complaint. It
 generally involves collection of documents, other evidence(s), obtaining statement of
 witness(es), their verification and scrutiny of the same to confirm if prima facie, misconduct
 exists. In common parlance, it is also referred to as Investigation. The investigation into a
 criminal offence is conducted by the CBI or any other Law Enforcement Agency (LEA)
 under the Cr.PC, 19731 . The preliminary enquiry is thus different from an investigation into
 criminal offence, as powers under the Cr.PC, 19731 are not vested in the authority
 conducting a preliminary enquiry / investigation.

3.1        AGENCIES FOR                    CONDUCTING               PRELIMINARY   ENQUIRY     /
           INVESTIGATION

3 .1.1     Central Bureau of Investigation / Anti-Corruption Bureau of Union
           Territories: As soon as a decision has been taken by the competent authority to
           cause investigation into the allegations contained in a complaint, it would be
           necessary to decide whether the allegations should be enquired within the
           organisation or whether a police investigation is necessary. As a general principle,
           investigation should be entrusted to CBI or to Anti Corruption Bureau (ACB) in the
           Union Territories (UTs) about allegations of the following nature:-
(i)        Allegations involving offences punishable under the law which the CBI is authorised
           to investigate; such as offences involving bribery, corruption, forgery, cheating,
           criminal breach of trust, falsification of records, etc.;
(ii)       Allegations of possession of assets disproportionate to known sources of income;
(iii)      Allegations of such nature in which the truth cannot be ascertained without making
           inquiries from non-official persons; or those involving examinationof non-government
            records, books of accounts, etc.;
(iv)       Allegations that need investigation abroad;
(v)        Allegations of a complicated nature requiring expert police investigation.
3.1.2      State Police Authorities : Allegations of such nature which indicate, prima facie, a
           criminal offence, but the offence is one which the CBI is not authorised to
           investigate, should be investigated by State Police Authorities.

3.1.3   Departmental Agency: Allegations relating to an irregularity other than a criminal
        offence, e.g. a departmental irregularity or act of negligence, violation of service
        rules / conditions and where the alleged facts are capable of verification or
        investigation within the organisation should be investigated departmentally.

3.1.4   Consultation with Central Bureau of Investigation: In certain cases, the

        would be appropriate to entrust the whole case to CBI.

3.2     PARALLEL INVESTIGATION BY THE DEPARTMENTAL AGENCY AND
        THE CENTRAL BUREAU OF INVESTIGATION

(i)     Once a case has been referred to and taken up by the CBI for investigation, a parallel
        investigation by departmental agencies should be avoided. In such cases, further action
        by the authorities within the organisation should be taken on completion of investigation
        by the CBI and on the basis of its report. However, the departmental agency is not
        precluded from investigating the non-criminal misconducts and if they decide to do so,
        the CBI should be informed of non-criminal aspects which the departmental agency has
        taken up for investigation. Further, if the departmental disciplinary proceedings have
        already been initiated on the basis of the investigation conducted by the departmental
        agencies, the administrative authorities may proceed with such disciplinary
        proceedings. In such cases, it would not be necessary for the CBI to investigate those
        allegations, which are the subject matter of the disciplinary proceedings, unless CBI
        observes criminal misconduct on the part of the offi.cial(s)concerned.

(ii)    On occasions, it is observed that if the CBI is investigating certain allegations against
        an official involving criminal misconduct, during the pendency of the CBI investigation,
        the organisation concerned does not take up investigation into other misconduct
        involving departmental irregularities, violation of service rules etc. It is to be noted that
        if required, the organisation concerned should investigate issues against the official
        concerned, which are not being investigated by the CBI and take the matter to logical
        conclusion as per extent procedure. Further, if the CBI is investigating criminal
        misconduct relating to a bank loan/ fraud/ forging of accounts, etc., the bank need not
        wait for the CBI to complete its investigation, to initiate action for recovery.

3.6        FORENSIC SCIENCE AS A TOOL FOR INVESTIGATION

(i)        Forensic science deals with scientific methods of investigation and collection of
           evidence. Normally, several branches of forensic science like forensic medicine,
           computer forensic, forensic pathology, forensic ballistics, etc., are employed for
           investigation of complex criminal cases. Forensic science can also be gainfully
           employed in investigations by administrative authorities, for collection of evidence,
           better appreciation of facts and fixing of responsibility.

(ii)       The organisations could explore improving the quality of their investigations
           through use of forensic tools and expertise for ascertaining facts that could be crucial
           for arriving at definite conclusions after investigation. In case of need, certain
           professional organisations may also be approached to help in application of forensic
           tools for investigation purpose.

(iii)      During investigation of fraud/ corruption cases, various forensic techniques are used

(ii)    With growing and widespread use of computer technology, a computing machine /
        computer system I communication network can be used for committing irregularities
        I crimes; at the same time, these objects can also be the target of nefarious activities.

(vi)    Services of other such forensic laboratories of the Central/ State Government or

4.1          EVOLUTION

             The Special Police Establishment (SPE) was set up in the year 1941 to investigate
             allegations of bribery and corruption in transactions of the War and Supply
             Department oflndia during World War-II. DSPE Act was brought into force in the
             year 1946. Subsequently, the CBI was set up through a Resolution dated
             01.04.1963 1 of the Government of India and SPE became a constituent of the CBI.
             Presently, the jurisdiction of the CBI extends to all departments of the Government
             of India, Union Territories and could be further extended to the states with the
             consent of the State Government concerned.

4.2          JURISDICTION OF THE CENTRAL BUREAU OF INVESTIGATION vis -
             a-vis ST ATE POLICE

             The CBI is empowered to investigate all offences notified by the Central
             Government under Section 3 of the DSPE Act, 1946. Though, the CBI's powers
             are concurrent and coextensive with those of the State Police Forces, it is not
             possible for the CBI to take up all such cases for investigation, given its limited
             resources. In order to bring clarity and to avoid duplication of efforts, an
             administrative arrangement has been arrived at, according to which:-

(i)          cases, which substantially and essentially concern the Central Government
             employees or the affairs of the Central Government, even though involving the State
             Government employees, are to be investigated by the CBI. The State Police is,
             however, kept informed of such cases and will render necessary assistance to the
             CBI during investigation;
(ii)         cases, which substantially and essentially involve the State Government employees
             or relate to the affairs of the State Government, even though involving certain
             Central Government employees, are investigated by the State Police. The CBI is
             informed of such cases and it extends assistance to the State Police during
             investigation, if necessary. When the investigation made by the State Police agencies
             involves a Central Government employee, the request for sanction for prosecution
             from the competent authority of the Central Government are routed through the CBI.

4.5.3   In some cases, it might become clear, soon after the enquiries are initiated, that

INTRODUCTION
On the basis of available records / evidence, the DA may decide for initiation of disciplinary
action against an official. The stage when a decision is taken by the DA to frame charges
against the delinquent official and a memorandum of charges is served on him, marks the
initiation of the process called disciplinary proceedings. It ends with the final orders of
punishment or exoneration being issued and served on the official concerned.


5.1         DISCIPLINARY RULES

5.1.1       The government has framed different sets of rn1es governing the recruitment and tbe
            conditions of service of government servants under the provisions of the Article 309
            and the Article 312 of the Constitution of India. Furth er, the governme nt has also
            made rules for regulating the conduct and discipline of government servants.

5 .1.2      The procedure for departmental disciplinary proceedings has been laid down in
            different sets of rules applicable to government servants. The rules having the widest
            applicability are the Central Civil Services (Classification , Control & Appeal) Rules,
            1965, [CCS (CCA) Rules, 1965] 1 and 2 which apply to every government servant
            including every civilian government servant in the Defence services, except:-

            "(a) any railway servant, as defined in Rule 102 of Volume I of the Indian
                  Railways Establishment Code,
            (b) any member of the All India Services,
            (c) any person in casual employment ,
            (d) any person subject to discharge from service on less than one month's notice ,
            (e) any person for whom special provision is made, in respect of matters covered
                by these rules, by or under any law for the time being in force or by or under
                any agreement entered into by or with the previous approval of the President
                 before or after the commencement of these rules, in regard to matters covered
                by such special provisions. "

5.2        PENALTIES

5.2.1      As per provisions contained under Rule 11 of CCS (CCA) Rules, 1965, the
           following penalties may, for good and sufficient reasons and as hereinafter provided ,
           be imposed on a government servant, namely:-
           Minor Pena lties -
           (a)   censure;
           (b)   withholding of his promotion;
           (c)   recovery from his pay of the whole or part of any pecuniary loss caused by
                 him to the government by negligence or breach of orders;
           (c 1) reduction to lower stage in the time-scale of pay by one stage for a period
                 not exceeding three years, without cumu lative effect and not adversely
                 affecting bis pension.
           (d)   withholding of increments of pay;

           Major Penalties -
           (e)   except for provisions, as mentioned in Para 5.2.1 (c 1) above, reduction to
                 a lower stage in the time scale of pay for a specified period, with further
                 directions as to whether or not the government servant will earn increments
                 of pay during the period of such reduction and whether on the expiry of
                 such period, the reduction wi ll or will not have the effect of postponing the
                 future increments of his pay;
           (f)   reduction to lower time-sca le of pay, grade, post or service for a period to
                 be specified in the order of penalty, which shall be a bar to the promotion
                 of the government servant during such specified period to the time-scale of
                 pay, grade, post or service from which he was reduced , with direction as to
                 whether or not, on promotion on the expiry of the said specified period -
                 ( 1) the period of reduction to time-scale of pay, grade, post or service shall
                 operate to postpone future increments of his pay, and if so, to what extent;
                 and
                 (2)the government servant shall regain his original seniority in the higher
                 time scale of pay, grade, post or service;
           (g)   compulsory retirement;
           (h)    removal from service which shall not be a disqualification for future
                 employment under the government;
           (i)   dismissal from service which shall ordinarily be a disqualification for future
                 employment under the government.

5.2.3      Warning: There may be occasions, when a supervisory / controlling officer may
           find it necessary to comment adversely on the work of an official subordinate to him
           (e.g. , point out negligence, carelessness , lack of thoroughness , delay, etc.) or he may
           call for an explanation for some act or omission and taking all factors into
           consideration, may come to a conclusion that while the matter is not serious enough
           to justify the imposition of the formal punishment of censure, it calls for some formal
           action, such as, the communication of warning, admonition , reprimand or caution.
           Administration of a warning in such circumstances does not amount to a fonnal
           punishment. It is an administrati ve device in the hands of the supervisory /
           controlling authority for conveying its criticism and disapproval of the work or
           conduct of the official concerned with a view to enable the official concerned to
           make an effort to remedy the defect, to impro ve efficiency and maintain discipline.

           Where, on conclusion of the departmental proceeding , it is considered that the
           Charged Officer (CO) deserves to be penalized, he should be awarded any of the
           statutory penalties mentioned in the Rules 11 of the CCS (CCA) Rules , 1965. In such
           a situation, a recordable warning should not be issued as it would, for all practica l
           purposes amount to a "Censure" which is a fonnal penalty to be imposed by a
           Competent Disciplinary Authority, after following the procedure prescribed in the
           disciplinary rules.

           A warning or reprimand, etc., may also be administered when as a result of a
           preliminary investigation or inquiry, if the Competent Authority comes to the
           conclusion that the conduct of the official is somewhat blameworthy, though not
           to the extent calling for the imposition of a formal penalty. In this regard , MHA's
           OM dated 15.12.1956 2 and DoPT' s OM dated 30.08.2022 3 may also be referred to.

5.6        COMMON PROCEEDINGS
5.6.1      Where two or more government servants are concerned in any case, the President
           or any other authority competent to impose the penalty of dismissal from service
           on all such government servants may make an order 1 directing that disciplinary

           competent to impose the penalty of dismissal on such government servants are

(i)        the authority which may function as the DA for the purpose of such common

(ii)       the penalties specified in Rule 11 of the CCS (CCA) Rules, 1965, which such

(iii)      whether the proceedings are to be initiated for imposition of a major penalty or
           a minor penalty.

           from government service and a person who is still in service, common proceedings
           against them cannot be instituted. The oral inquiry against both of them could be
           entrusted to the same inquiring authority.
5.6.3      A joint proceeding against the accused and accuser is an irregularity which should
           be avoided.

5. 7       SPECIAL PROCEDURE IN CERTAIN CASES
5.7.1      Rule 19 of the CCS (CCA) Rules, 1965, provides that notwithstanding anything
           contained in Rules 14 to 18 of the CCS (CCA) Rules, 1965: -
(i)        where any penalty is imposed on a government servant on the ground of
           conduct which has led to his conviction on a criminal charge; or
(ii)       where the DA is satisfied for reasons to be recorded by it in writing, that it is
           not reasonably practicable to hold an inquiry in the manner provided in the CCS
           (CCA) Rules, 1965; or
(iii)      where the President is satisfied that in the interest of the security of the State, it
           is not expedient to hol.d any inquiry in the manner provided in the CCS (CCA)
           Rules, 1965, the DA may consider the circumstances of the case and make such
           orders thereon as it deem s fit.
(iv)       The government servant may be given an opportunity to make a representation
           on the penalty proposed to be imposed before any order is passed in a case as
           stated in Para 5.7.1 (i) above. The UPSC shall also be consulted where such
           consultation is necessary before any order is passed in a case, as stated in Para
           5.7 .1 (i) above.

5.7.2      In a case where a government servant has been convicted by a Court of Law for any
           penal offence but dealt with under Section 3 or 4 of the Probation of Offenders Act,
           1958, he shall not suffer any disqualification because of the provisions of Section 12
           of the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958 which reads as follows: -
           "Notwithstanding anything contained in any other law, a person found guilty of an
           offence and dealt with under the provisions of Section 3 or Section 4 shall not suffer
           disqualification if any, attaching to a conviction of an offence under such law:
           Provided that nothing in the section shall apply to a person who, after his release
           under Section 4, is subsequently sentenced for the original offence" .
           The question whether action under Rule 19(i) of the CCS (CCA) Rules, can be taken
           against a government servant, who, though convicted by a Court of Law but is not
           to suffer any disqualification because be has been dealt with under Section 3 or 4
           of the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958 has been considered in consultation with
           the Ministry of Law and on the basis of the Andhra Pradesh High Court's Judgement
           in A. Satyanarayana Murthy Vs. Zonal Manager, L.I.C 1 .

 5.7.3       Dispensation of inquiry under Rule 19(iii) of the CCS (CCA) Rules , 1965:
             In cases where an inquiry is to be dispensed with in the interest of the security of the
             State under Rule 19(iii) of the CCS (CCA) Rules , 1965, the order of the Presiden t
             should be obtained in such cases. For this purpose , it would be sufficient if the
             order s of the Minister-in-charge are obtained , as the Supreme Court, in Shamsher
             Singh's Case (AlR 1974 SC 2192) has overruled its earlier decision in the case of
             Sardari Lal Vs. Union oflndia and others (Civil Appeal No.576 of 1969, [1971 AJR
             154 7, 1971 SCR (3) 461] under which it has been ruled that each such case has to be
             submitted to the President, for orders. The Supreme Court has now clearly pointed
             out that the Rules of Business and the allocation among the minister s of the said
             business, indicate that the rules of business made under the Article 77 (3) in the case
             of the President and the A1ticle 166 (3) in the case of the Governor of the State is the
             decision of the President or the Governor respectively. In the said judgment it has
             been held that neither the President nor the Governor has to exercise the executive
             function s personally. It would thus be clear that the requirement of proviso ( c) to the
             Article 311 (2) of the Constitution and Rule 19 (iii) of the CCS (CCA) Rules , 1965
             would be satisfied if the matter is submitted to the Minister- in-charge under the
             relevant rule s of business and it receives the approval of the Minister.

5.10.2   A charge may be described as an accusation, relating to which, prima-facie there are
         enough specific fact(s) and evidence(s), to be taken seriously. A charge may be
         related to negligence in the performance of official duties , inefficienc y, acceptance
         of sub-standard work, false measurement of work executed, execution of work below
         specification, breach of a conduct rule, etc. A charge should briefly, clearly and
         precisely identify the misconduct / misbehaviour. It should also specify the time,
         place, persons or things involved so that the government servant concerned has clear
         notice of his involvement.

5.10.3   The article(s) of charge should be framed with great care. The following guidelines
         would be of help: -
(i)      Each charge should be expressed in clear and precise tem1s; it should not be
         vague;
(ii)     If a transaction / event amounts to more than one type of misconduct, then each
         misconduct should be mentioned;
(iii)    If a transaction / event shows that the government servant must be guilty of one
         or the other of misconduct, depending on one or the other set of circumstan.ces,
         then the charge can be in the alternative;
(iv)     A separate charge should be framed in respect of each separate transaction / event or
         a series of related transactions / events amounting to misconduct or mi sbehaviour;
(v)      Multiplication or splitting up of charges on the basis of the same allegation should
         be avoided;
(vi)     The wording of the charge should not appear to be an expression of opinion as
         to the guilt of the accused;
(vii)    A charge should not relate to a matter which has already been the subject matter
         of an inquiry and decision , unless it is based on benefit of doubt or on technical
         considerations;
(viii)   A charge should not refer to the report of Preliminary Investigation           or the

5 .10.4 Special care should be taken while drafting a charge-sheet. A charge related to lack

        mere inclusion of a statement of hypoth etical or tentative conclusion of guilt in the
        charge, will not , by itself, vitiate the charge-sheet 4 •


5.11        STATEMENT OF IMPUTATIONS

            The statement of imputations should give a full and precise recitation of the specific
            and relevant acts of commission or omission on the part of the CO in support of each
            article of charge including any admission or confession made by the CO and any
            other circumstance which is proposed to be taken into consideration . A statement
            that a CO allowed certain entries to be made with ulterior motive was held to be
            much too vague. A vague accusation that the CO was in the habit of doing certain
            acts in the past is not sufficient. The statement of imputations should be precise and
            factual. In particular, in cases of any misconduct / misbehaviour, it should mention
            the conduct / behaviour expected or the rule violated. It would be improper to call
            an Investigating Officer's Report, a statement of imputations . While drafting the
            statement of imputations , it would not be proper to mention the defence and enter
            into a discussion on the merits of the case. Wording of the imputations should be
            clear enough to justify the imputations even in the light of the likely version of the
            CO concerned .

5.12       LIST OF WITNESSES

           A number of witnesses are usually examined during the course of the preliminary
           inquiry and their stateme nts are recorded. The list of such witnesses should be
           carefully checked. Only those witnesses who would be able to give evidence to
           substantiate the allegations, during the oral inquiry, should be included in the
           state ment. Formal witnesses to produce only documents, need not be mentioned in
           the list of witnesses.

5.13       LIST OF DOCUMENTS

           The documents containing evidence in support of the allegations which are propo sed
           to be listed for production during the inquiry, should be carefully scrutinised. All
           material particulars given in the allegations, such as dates, names, makes, figures,
           total of amount, etc., should be carefully checked with reference to the original
           documents and records.

5.18.1        The DA has the inherent power to review and modify the articles of charge after receipt

              rule (4) of Rule 14 of the CCS (CCA) Rules, 1965.

5.18.2        During the course of inquiry, if it is found necessary to amend the charge-sheet , it is
              permissible for the DA to do so, provided a fresh opportunity is given to the CO in
              respect of the amended / supplementary charge-sheet. The IA may hold the inquiry
              again from the stage considered necessary so that the CO has a reasonable opportunity
              to subm it his defence or produce his witness(es) in respect of the amended charge-
              sheet. However, if there is a major change in the charge-sheet, it would be desirable
              to hold fresh proceedings on the basis of the amended charge-sheet.

5.19        APPOINTMENT OF INQUIRING AUTHORITY
5.19.1      If the DA finds that any or all the charges have not been admitted by the CO in bis
            written statement of defence or ifno written statement of defence is received by him
            by the date specified, the DA may itself inquire into such charges or appoint an IA
            to inquire into the truth of the charges. Though the CCS (CCA) Rules, 1965 permit
            such an inquiry to be made by the DA itself, the normal practice is to appoint another
            official as IA. Tt should be ensured that the official so appointed has no bias and has
            had no occasion to express an opinion in the earlier stages of the case.

5.19.2      Having regard to the nature and gravity of the disciplinary case, inquiry may be
            entrusted to an official of the Commission, designated as the 'Commissioner                                    for
            Departmental Inquiry (CDI)'.

5.19.3      Inquiring Authority to be sufficiently senior : It may not be always possible to
            entrust inquirie s against the CO to Gazetted Officers . However, the inquiries shou ld
            be conducted by an IA who is sufficiently senior to the official whose conduct is
            being inquired into, as inquiry by a junior official cannot command the confidence
            which it deserves. Further, it may be ensured that only an official with sufficient
            service experience and seniority is appointed as the Presenting Officer (PO).

5.20       ASSISTANCE  TO THE     CHARGED                                             OFFICER               IN       THE
           PRESENTATION OF HIS CASE

5.20.1     In the copy of the order appointing the PO, endorsed to the CO, the CO should be
           asked to finalise the selection of his Defence Assistant before the commencement
           of the proce edings. The CO may avail himself of the assistance of any other
           government servant, as defined in CCS(CCA) Rules, 19651, posted in any office
           either at the headquarters or at the place where inquiry is to be held. The CO may
           take the assistance of any other government servant posted at any other station if
           the IA, having regard to the circumstances of the case and for reasons to be recorded
           in writing, so permits .

5 .20 .2 If the PO appointed by the DA is a legal practitioner, the CO will be so informed
         by the DA as soon as the PO is appointed, so that the CO may, if he so desires,
         engage a legal practitioner to present the case on his behalf before the IA. The CO
         may not otherwise engage a legal practitioner unless the DA, having regard to the
         circumstances of a case, so permits. If, for examp le, the facts and the mass of
         evidence are very complicated and a layperson is not able to understand the
         implications thereof and prepare a proper defence, the facility of a lawyer may be
         allowed to the CO as a part of the reasonable opportunit y, subject to provisions in
         the relevant rules.

5.20.3     There may be good and sufficient circumstances for the DA to exercise his
           discretion in favour of the CO to allow him to be represented by a legal practitioner.
           For examples, when on behalf of the DA, the case is being represented by a PO of
           the CBI or by a government law officer i.e. Legal Advisor, Junior Legal Advisor
           etc. In such cases, any exercise of discretion against allowing a legal practitioner to
           repre sent the CO, is likely to be held by the court as arbitrary and prejudicial to the
           defence of the CO.

5.20.4     No permission is needed by the CO to secure the assistance of any other government
           servant. The latter also is not required to take pem1ission for assisting the CO. It
           would, however, be necessary for him to obtain the permission of his controlling
           authority to absent himself from office in order to assist the CO during the inquiry .

5.20.5     CO involved in disciplinary proceedings may also take the assistance of retired
           government servants. Details in this regards are given in Chapter 16 of Handbook
           for Inquiry Officers and Disciplinary Authorities issued by DoPT on 26.12.2024 2 •

5.22       HOLDING ORAL INQUIRY

5.22.1     Stages of oral inquiry : The main stages of an oral inquiry are as under:
(i)        Pre Hearing Stage: From the appointment of IA and PO till the commencement of
           hearing. During this stage, the IA and PO examine the documents received by them
           and ensure their correctness. Besides , the PO prepares for the presentation of the
           case.
(ii)       Preliminary Hearing Stage: From the time the parties start appearing before the IA,
           till the commencement of presentation of evidence. During this stage, CO is asked
           once again as to whether the charges are admitted, inspection of documents take
           place and CO presents the list of documents and oral witnesses required for the
           purpose of defence.

(iii)      Regular Hearing Stage: During this stage, evidence is produced by the parties and
           examination-in-chief and cross examination of witness( es) is done.

(iv)       Post Hearing Stage: During this stage, the PO and the CO submit their written briefs
           to the IA and the IA submits his report to the DA. Detailed procedure for conduct
           of oral inquiry is given in Chapter 17 of ''Handbook for Inquiry Officers and
           Disciplinary Authorities" issued by DoPT on 26.12.2024 1 .

           conduct the inquiry proceedings at a location taking into account the availability of
           records, station / place where the misconduct occurred, as well as the convenience

(ii)        The date for preliminary hearing should be chosen in such a way so as to provide

            posted outstation, date of hearing must be so fixed that there is adequate time for

            entertained.
(xi)        Summons to witnesses should be issued well in advance. The PO and the CO should

5.22.8     Conducting Ex parte proceedings: If the CO does not subm it the written
           statement of defence on or before the date specified for the purpose or does not
           appear in person before the IA or otherwise fails or refuses to comply with the
           provisions of the applicable rules, the IA may hold the inquiry Ex parte. If the CO
           does not take advantage of the opportunity given to him to explain any facts or
           circumstances which appear against him, he has only him self to blame and the IA
           has no choice but to proceed Ex parte . But if a CO under suspension pleads his
           inability to attend the inquiry on account of financial stringency caused by the
           non payment of subsistence allowance to him, the proceedings conducted against
           him Ex parte would be violative of the provisions of the Article 311 (2) of the
           Constitution as the CO did not receive a reasonable opportunity of defending himself
           in the disciplinary proceedings . This view has been upheld by the Supreme Court in
           the case of Ghan Shyam Das Srivastava vs. State of Madhya Pradesh 1. Therefore, in
           cases where recourse to Ex parte proceeding becomes necessary, it should be
           checked and confirmed that the CO's inability to attend the inquiry is not because
           of non-payment of subsistence allowance .

5.22.9     Manner of Ex parte proceedings: In Ex parte proceeding, the full inquiry has to
           be held i.e., the PO will produce documentary evidence and witnesses in the manner
           outlined in Para 5.22.1 above. Notice of each hearing should be sent to the CO also.
           However, if the CO joins the proceedings at a later stage, he can not be prevented
           from doing so.

(i)        "Corruption cannot be eliminated or even significantly reduced unless

           social, economic and educative measures" (Report of Committee on
           Prevention of Corruption, 1964) .

(ii)       During a debate in Parliament in June, 1962, Members of Parliament
           expressed concern over com1ption in public administration and sought
           remedial measures. In response, a Committee on 'Prevention of
           Corruption' was set up under Shri K. Santhanam , Member of Parl iament

(a)        administrativedelays,
(b)        government raking upon itself more than what it could manage by way of

(c)        scope for personal discretion in the exercise of powers vested in different

           importance to citizens in their day-to-day affairs.

           than merely taking action when some case comes to notice. It was

           which citizens come into contact with the organisations andwhy. It was also
           recommended that a study should be made by the ministries of the extent,

 7.2        THE CONCEPT OF PREVENTIVE VIGILANCE

 (i)        Concept:
            It is adoption of a package of measures to improve systems and procedures to
            eliminate / significantly reduce corruption, promote transparency and ensure ease
            of doing business.

 (ii)       Who is required to implement preventive vigilance measures?
            Preventive vigilance involves systemic improvements which, besides reducing
            corruption also leads to better operational results. It is a tool of management for
            good governance and therefore, it is the duty of the management as a whole, and
            not of the CVO alone. fudeed, it can be said that it is the duty of every official.

 7.2.1      Causes of corruption: Corruption is not merely failure of an individual, it is often
            a failure of the system, controls and organisation culture. Preventive vigilance is
            aimed at identifying and addressing the root cause of corruption within the
            organisation. The common causes of corruption, inter alia, could be:-

 (i)        Excessive regulations & licensing;
 (ii)       Complicated rules and procedures;
 (iii)      Monopoly over delivery of goods, services etc.;
 (iv)       Lack of transparency;
 (v)        Lack of accountability;
 (vi)       Excessive discretionary powers;
 (vii)      Poor regulatory framework;
 (viii)     Poor grievance redressal mechanism;
 (ix)       Very low rate of detection of corruption;
 (x)        Lack of condemnation of corrupt practices by the public;
 (xi)       Absence of a formal system of inculcating values, ethics and integrity;
 (xii)      fuadequacy of regular/ periodic / surprise checks;
 (xiii)     Rigid bureaucratic framework/ processes;
 (xiv)      Lack of awareness about rules, laws, rights, duties, procedure to lodge complaints
            etc.

 7.3        POTENTIAL AREAS OF CORRUPTION

            Preventive vigilance is aimed at tackling the areas vulnerable to corruption within
            the organisation. Although potential areas of corruption are specific to organisations
            I sectors, there are some broad areas common to all organisations, which need
            special attention while putting in place a system of preventive vigilance. These
            relate to: -
 (i)        Procurement: Procurement is a vast area ranging from procurement of store

          Preventive vigilance measures can broadly be categorized as: -

  (i)     Simplification and standardisation of rules and procedures: Simplification and

          arbitrariness in decision making process, which in tum reduces corruption.
          Identifying areas involving exercise of discretion which are not governed by
          guidelines, together with a complete review of existing rules and regulations needs
          to be undertaken to introduce clarity and accountability. Similarly, simplification
          and standardisation of forms / applications also reduces scope for corruption.

  (ii)    Leveraging technology:

 7.5       PUBLIC PARTICIPATION IN PROMOTING INTEGRITY
           AND ERADICATING CORRUPTION

 7. 5 .1   "In the long run, the fight against corruption will succeed only to the extent of which
            a favourable social climate is created. When such a climate is created and
            corruption becomes abhorrent to the minds of the public and the public servants
            and social controls become effective, other administrative, disciplinruy and punitive
            measures may become unimportant and may be relaxed and reduced to a
            minimum". The Santhanam Committee, set up in the year 1962 to study important
            aspects on the evils of corruption in Indian society made the above observations
            about the role of society at large in the fight against corruption, which holds true to
            the day.

 7. 5 .2   Promoting integrity and eradication of corruption cannot be achieved only by the
           efforts of agencies, without the active support and participation of the citizens and
           social institutions. Public participation plays a vital role in the fight against
           corruption in:-

 (i)       Encouraging ethical conduct of the individual official and the organisation;
 (ii)      Educating and creating awareness about the rights and duties of the citizens; rules,
           regulations, duties and responsibilities of the officials and public institutions;
           especially in implementing various government welfare schemes;
 (iii)     Acting as a 'watchdog' through public scrutiny of the actions of public servants
           by exposing the wrongdoers and standing by the upright and honest officials;
 (iv)      Acting as a feedback channel to the public authority about grievance redressal
           mechanism meant for the common man;
 (v)       Institutional and moral support to those who are fighting the corrupt officials ;
 (vi)      Exhorting the citizens and the organisations to perform their lawful duties.

 7.5.3     Encouraging ethical conduct:
 (i)       Corruption mainly includes abuse of authority and selfish exercise of power
           by those who hold special position in public life. Hence, corruption can be linked
           to lack of ethical values. Combating corruption is, therefore, not just a matter of
           making laws and creating institutions, but is deeply rooted in human values, ethics
           and morality of the individuals, organisations and the society at large. Inculcating
           ethical and moral values in the citizens i.e. truthfulness, honesty, integrity, probity,
           courage, uprightness, respect for and obedience to law, etc. is the foundation stone
           of any society's fight against corruption. Stigmatising the culture of corruption,

            favouritism, nepotism and promoting meritocracy creates a conducive social
            climate. Avarice and craving for easy money is to be avoided. If the citizen is
            taught to say 'no to bribe', the supply side of conuption automatically gets stifled.
 (ii)       Parents, family, peer group, teachers, educational institutions, social intellectual
            and spiritual leaders, civil society, press, mass media including social media,
            governmental and Non-Governmental Organisations (NGOs), etc. have a major
            role to play in the inculcation and dissemination of high ethical and moral values
            in individuals, organisations and the society at large.

 7.6        Participation of Institutions:

            the special efforts of honest and upright public officials.

 (ii)       Social media: The use of electronic social media has become very popular. The

 (iv)       Leaders: The intellectual, social and spiritual leaders can also play their part in
            eradicating conuption. Through their teachings and work, they can instil
            virtuosity, noble values, high moral and ethical standards among their followers.

 (v)        Civil society: Civil society can contribute to a nation's fight against conuption

         neither take nor offer bribe, to perform all tasks with honesty and transparency, act
         in public interest and report any incident of corruption to appropriate authority.

 (iii)   Similarly, by taking the futegrity Pledge, organisations i.e. corporate / entities I
         firms, etc., would affirm their commitment to eradicate corruption and to uphold
         highest standards of integrity and good governance by promoting a culture of
         honesty and integrity in the conduct of their activities. Organisations would pledge
         to neither offer nor accept bribe, commit to good corporate governance based on
         transparency, accountability and fairness, adhere to relevant laws, rules and
         compliance mechanisms in the conduct of business, adopt a code of ethics for all
         its employees, sensitise their employees oflaws, regulations, etc., relevant to their
         work for honest discharge of their duties, provide grievance redressal and 'Whistle
         Blower' mechanisms for reporting grievances and fraudulent activities and
         protect the rights and interests of stake holders and the society at large.

         The Commission acknowledges citizens and organisation taking the futegrity
         Pledge.

7.12    INTEGRITY PACT
(i)     futegrity Pact (IP) is an important tool of preventive vigilance which is aimed at
        preventing corruption and ensuring integrity in public procurement. The
        Commission is the nodal authority for implementation of IP in fudia. It addresses
        not only bribery, but also other corrupt practices such as collusion and bid rigging.
        IP is a written agreement between the government organisation and all the bidders
        agreeing to refrain themselves from bribery, collusion, etc. If the written
        agreement is violated, IP describes the sanctions that shall apply.

(ii)    IP has a monitoring system which provides for independent oversight. The
        Commission nominates fudependent External Monitors (IEMs) to monitor
        implementation of IP . Detailed guidelines for effective implementation of IP are
        issued by the Commission separately and are available on the Commission's
        website i.e. www.cvc.gov.in.

7.13    MANAGING CONFLICT OF INTEREST

(i)     Conflict of interest flows from the principle of natural justice that 'No one should
        be the judge in his/ her own case (Nemo judex in causa sua)'. It leads to bias. Bias
        means an act which leads to unfair activity whether in a conscious or unconscious
        manner in relation to the party or a cause or case. A conflict of interest occurs when
        an official's personal interests - family, friendships, financial, or social- could
        compromise his judgment, decisions or actions in the workplace. It arises when an
        official is involved in a particular matter as part of his official duties with an outside
        organisation with which he also has a financial interest, i.e., the officials (a) spouse,
        (b) children & other relations, (c) general partner, (d) an organisation in which the
        official serves as its chief, officer, director, trustee, partner, or employee, etc. or (e)
        a person or organisation with which the official is negotiating for prospective or
        has an arrangement for prospective employment.
(ii)    fu such a situation, an official's private interests could improperly influence the
        performance of official duties. Conflict of interest reduces public trust and
        confidence in the integrity and impartiality of public functionaries.
(iii)   Conduct rules, other statutes or guidelines governing the service conditions of
        public servants appropriately address the conflict-of-interest issue. Prompt action

            should be taken for violation of such rules, statutes or guidelines.

 (iv)       Conflicts can be real or apparent. A real conflict exists when an official participates
            personally and substantially in particular matters that have a direct and predictable
            effect on a :financialinterest of the official or one of the five 'others' listed in sub-
            para (i) above. On the other hand, an appearance of a conflict exists when an
            official is involved in a particular matter involving specific outside parties
            (including individual, corporate entities, etc.) and the circumstances are such that
            a reasonable person with knowledge of the relevant facts would question the
            official's impartiality in the matter. Such circumstances include the involvement
            of a relative, employer of spouse, or former employer in the matter.

 (v)        Conflict of interest for officials have been elaborately addressed in the conduct
            rules e.g. AIS (Conduct) Rules, 1968, CCS (Conduct) Rules, 1964, The Railway
            Services (Conduct) Rules, 1966, CDA Rules of other government organisations
            and also other statutory provisions etc.

 (vi)       Apart from the provisions in the service rules, some other measures for addressing
            issue of conflict of interest are enlisted below:-

 (a)        CVOs who head the Vigilance Unit of an organisation are normally appointed
            from outside that organisation.
 (b)        CV Os are not assigned any operational duties, which involve :financial
            implications like procurement, etc. or have a vigilance sensitivity.
 (c)        CEOs of CPSEs / PSBs / PSI Cs / PSFis are not entrusted with additional charge
            of vigilance units as they deal with administrative / :financialmatters.
 (d)        On ceasing to hold office, a Chairperson or a Member of Central Vigilance
            Commission, Human Rights Commission, Lokpal, Telecom Regulatory
            Authority of India and other such oversight or regulatory bodies are ineligible for
            further employment under the Government of India or under the Government of
            any State (i.e., to any office of profit).
 (e)        e-govemance has been implemented to a large extent in order to reduce discretion
            and conflict of interest.
 (f)        Officials holding sensitive posts in Central Government organisations are rotated
            at regular intervals.
 (g)        In Departmental Promotion Committees, every member is required to furnish
            a certificate to the Chairperson of the committee before commencement of the
            meeting, stating that none of his close relatives are being considered by the
            committee and he otherwise also is not interested in any candidate.
